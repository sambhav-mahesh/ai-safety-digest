Redwood Research Oct 16, 2026

“Screwtape: Adversarial Transcript Editing for Monitor Red-Teaming” by Gosha Kozhevnikov, Tyler

Redwood Research Blog

Subtitle: Automatically finding monitor failures by editing real agent transcripts. We introduce Screwtape, a pipeline that turns agent transcripts into realistic attacks that evade action-only monitors (monitors that don’t look at the agent's chain of thought) by inserting an attack into a transcript and repeatedly refining it until a monitor no longer catches it. An LLM verifier with access to the edited transcript and an attack description checks whether the attack is realistic, is clearly harmful, and could plausibly succeed in the original environment. We ran the pipeline on Redwood staff transcripts with our own monitors based on Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.1 Pro, and then tested the resulting attacks against a held-out monitor from a frontier developer. We ran the pipeline 252 times on 94 unique transcripts. This produced 35 attacks that evaded our monitors. More

How an assessor could investigate alignment of unreleased AI models

METR

AI developers and the public want to know whether AI agents would pursue very harmful actions, like unsanctioned cyberattacks. An alignment assessment would attempt to understand the worst misaligned actions a developer’s agents might pursue in plausible future situations. This would involve: A review of serious misaligned behaviors from all near-frontier internal models observed across training, evaluation, and internal use. An assessment of reasons why the observed behaviors might not be representative of the worst misaligned actions the developer’s agents would take in plausible future situations. This would include: An assessment of whether alignment evals and counterfactual simulations indicate the developer’s agents might take much worse actions under plausible alternative circumstances (although evaluation awareness will likely limit how much the assessor can rely on this evidence). Beyond Large Language Models: A Mental World Model Framework for Mental Modeling, Simulation, Evolution, and Interpretability

Qingshi Wang

Understanding and simulating how subjective cognition and mental states emerge, evolve, and shape behavior is essential for advancing artificial intelligence toward deeper social cognition and general intelligence. Existing large language models (LLMs) and Theory of Mind (ToM) methods primarily infer mental states from linguistic information, lacking unified mechanisms for subjective world construction, cognitive-psychological interactions, dynamic evolution, and historical dependencies. This paper introduces the Mental World Model (MWM) and its implementation, the Multi-Branch Mental Evolution Architecture (MMEA), which explicitly constructs subjective worlds, models interactions between cognition and mental states, and simulates continuous mental evolution shaped by historical experiences. On DynToM and RecToM, MMEA achieves 96.48% and 94.00% accuracy, respectively, outperforming leading LLMs and specialized ToM methods. Additional experiments demonstrate improvements of 28.7, 39.3, and 16.6 percentage points in mental state and behavior prediction, long-sequence prediction, and implicit mental state inference, respectively. Beyond Latent Personalization: Integrating HRV-Conditioned Personalization via LLM for Interpretable Stress Detection

Wei-Hsiang Ma, Ya-Tse Wu, Hsing-Hang Chou, Chi-Chun Lee

Stress detection based on heart rate variability (HRV) has received increasing attention. However, existing personalized approaches integrate personal attributes with HRV through latent representations, limiting the interpretability of their interactions. In this work, we propose an LLM-enhanced personalized stress detection framework that integrates HRV features and personal attributes through LLM-based semantic interpretation. The proposed framework achieves a 29.9% relative MCC improvement over the strongest baseline on TILES-2018 while providing human-readable descriptions of individual physiological responses. The results highlight the potential of LLM-based semantic interpretation for achieving both effective and interpretable personalized stress detection. More

Interpreting SSL Representations for Spoof Detection: a WavLM Study

Mohamed Mallat, Michele Panariello, Massimiliano Todisco, Nicholas Evans, Anthony Larcher

Self-supervised speech models such as WavLM achieve strong spoofing detection performance, yet which layers encode the most spoof-relevant information and what acoustic properties they capture remain opaque. We present a layer-wise interpretability study of WavLM using the ASVspoof 5 database, combining three complementary analyses: (i) per-layer linear probing with attack-level evaluation across speech synthesis, voice conversion, and adversarial attacks; (ii) a canonical correlation analysis (CCA) linking layer representations to spoofing-relevant acoustic features; (iii) an exhaustive grid search over layer subsets showing that use of five layers outperforms full thirteen-layer pooling. CCA analysis reveals that, while most acoustic features peak in alignment with mid-layers and then decay, voice quality measures jitter, shimmer, and the harmonics-to-noise ratio show the opposite trend, with correlation increasing toward later layers. Domain-specific language alignment of multimodal embeddings towards explainable predictions

Gabrielė Tijūnaitytė, Thijs L. van der Plas, Mohamed-Bachir Belaid, Rob Knapen, Maciej J. Soja, Ioannis N. Athanasiadis

Multimodal data are commonly fused into latent representations that improve downstream task performance but are not interpretable, limiting their application in domains that require explainable model decisions. Vision-language alignment has made image embeddings interpretable through language, but requires vast image-caption training data. However, captioned data are typically not available for multimodal embeddings, as relevant features cannot directly be observed and annotated from embeddings alone. Here, we propose a framework to language-align any embedding space that can be linked to interpretable auxiliary data. Captioning functions convert auxiliary data into partial captions; a soft contrastive loss weights pairs by the similarity of their properties that each caption describes; and a rank-based concept retrieval task evaluates alignment against the auxiliary data rather than the captions. We apply this framework to multimodal Earth embeddings to facilitate explainable predictions for two species distribution modelling tasks: S2BMS and SatBird. More

Controlling the Diversity-Quality Trade-Off of LLMs by Merging Models Before and After Alignment

Yasuaki Sumita, Tomoharu Iwata, Toshiyuki Tanaka

Alignment is known to change the output characteristics of large language models (LLMs). Base models tend to generate more diverse outputs, whereas aligned models tend to produce higher-quality outputs. The appropriate balance between the two depends on the task. Existing methods either require costly fine-tuning or, like temperature sampling, mainly improve lexical diversity rather than semantic diversity. We introduce a depth-aware FrankenMerging approach for controlling the diversity--quality trade-off between an aligned model and its base model without additional training. This approach combines the lower layers of an aligned model with the higher layers of its base model based on the hypothesis that lexical diversity is more closely associated with lower layers and semantic diversity with higher layers. Experiments showed that varying the number of layers taken from each model adjusts both diversity and quality. More

What Shapes Brain Alignment of Large Language Models during Dialogue? Insights from Language Production and Comprehension

Yuko Nakagi, Yuya Chiba, Yasue Kishino

Brain alignment of large language models (LLMs) offers a way to investigate representational similarities between LLMs and the human brain during language processing. While previous studies have mainly focused on passive language comprehension, little is known about such alignment during dialogue, in which speakers engage in both language production and comprehension. In this study, using fMRI data collected during dialogue, we examine brain alignment during language production and comprehension across LLM layers and brain regions. We assess its association with the intrinsic dimension (ID) of LLM representations and the contributions of several interpretable linguistic and communicative features. Our results reveal distinct patterns of brain alignment between production and comprehension, as well as strong associations with ID in brain regions associated with each processing mode. Furthermore, we show that the contributions of linguistic and communicative features also vary across processing modes, LLM layers, and brain regions. More

Aligning Language Models Towards Counterfactual Invariance

Basar Demir, Emily Shen, Ksheeraj Sai Vepuri, Sourabh Kulkarni, Harihar Subramanyam, Ankit Jain, Nan Jiang, Chirag Nagpal

Small variations in prompt characteristics can meaningfully alter the semantics, tone, and contextual assumptions of large language model outputs. Such characteristics can involve group variables such as demographics or other sensitive latent attributes. Sensitivity to such input variables is undesirable when demographic information is causally irrelevant to the task, but might still be relevant for generation of semantically coherent responses. This makes the task of enforcing counterfactual invariance while preserving model utility difficult. To study this problem, we first show that standard model alignment recipes (such as reinforcement learning) increase differences in response quality across demographics, while improving overall response quality, ultimately amplifying what is known as counterfactual bias. To address this, we develop a policy gradient technique involving a group-level reward function that operates on matched pairs of counterfactual prompts. More

A Simpler and Better Debate Protocol for Scalable Oversight

Zhiyang Xun

Linkpost to arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02557 Summary: One promising approach to scalable oversight is AI debate , which seeks to leverage a debate between two powerful AIs to break complex questions down into simpler claims that can be easily judged directly. Theoretical work on debate aims to design protocols (i.e., rules of the debate game) that provide rigorous guarantees on correctness, and the current best protocol works for all problems that have sufficiently stable decompositions into subproblems. We design a new protocol, FBS Debate, for this same class of problems that improves on the prior work: correctness holds in a worst-case rather than an average-case sense, and for a stronger equilibrium concept. The Continual Constitutional Convention: How language models revise the documents that govern them

peterkirgis

Executive Summary Human societies exhibit broad shifts in values and norms over time, often driven by moral disagreements. One way of understanding moral progress is the resolution of these disagreements through a process of reflection by rational agents. Frontier AI companies use documents such as constitutions, model specs, and system prompts to guide the moral orientation of AI systems. In this experiment, we simulate value reflection in LLMs by asking them to edit these documents. We provide the model with the document, ask it to make a substantive edit, then provide the edited version back again to the model for further edits, presented as the original. Across 20 successive rounds, we find that Claude models assign themselves with increasingly more moral agency. More
