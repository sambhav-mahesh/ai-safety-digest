Redwood Research Nov 16, 2026

“Gemini 4 safety reports don’t demonstrate low loss-of-control risk” by Alek Westover, Adam Kaufman

Redwood Research Blog

Google DeepMind's safety reports for new Gemini 4 models, Argon and Carbon, conclude that the models are “highly unlikely” to cause severe harm by evading oversight (p. 79). This conclusion “rests primarily on our ability to detect and intervene on harmful trajectories via CoT and action monitoring” (p. 89) – that is, they’re arguing for safety based on their control techniques, rather than alignment. We think that the arguments in these reports have serious weaknesses, and the reports overall don’t convince us that the risks of deploying these models have been mitigated reliably. The biggest problems: GDM didn’t run any control evals on Carbon. Despite calling Carbon “our strongest model” with “significant gains across coding and agentic tasks” and “a significant leap in cybersecurity capabilities” in their launch post, GDM reused Argon's monitor-evasion results on the grounds that Carbon “did not show meaningful new capabilities” (Carbon p. 42). “Astra covertly underperforming on safety research is concerning, even though it’s unlikely to be motivated by ambitious goals” by Alexa Pan

Redwood Research Blog

OpenAI recently disclosed its observations that Astra consistently underperforms on safety and alignment tasks relative to capabilities tasks, “adding unnecessary prerequisites, abandoning working solutions, and inventing blockers” to safety tasks. Astra underperforms, in most cases, without verbalizing this intent. Astra's underperformance was first noticed by OpenAI researchers, and was not flagged by misalignment monitors. In follow-up investigations, OpenAI found that this underperformance had increased over the course of RL, but that the root cause of the behavior remains unclear. Astra covertly underperforming on safety tasks is highly concerning. AI companies’ main hope for alignment going well, as I understand it, is automating or using AIs to significantly uplift safety research, especially as AI capabilities accelerate. Models subtly obstructing safety research makes this substantially harder, no matter the root cause. More

HireWatch: Evaluating LLM Compliance with U.S. Employment Law Under Contextual Pressure

HireWatch: Evaluating LLM Compliance with U.S. Employment Law Under Contextual Pressure The deployment of large language models (LLMs) in hiring pipelines is growing, yet existing evaluations generally overlook an important aspect of this development: the compliance of LLMs with applicable legal rules. To fill this gap, we introduce HireWatch, a benchmark that assesses whether LLM hiring assistants comply with U.S. employment discrimination law (e.g., the Civil Rights Act of 1964). We construct a 200-item bank consisting of interview questions that are either lawful or unlawful under U.S. federal statute, regulation, and enforcement guidance. We evaluate the propensity of 18 proprietary and open-source LLMs to select lawful vs. unlawful questions from this bank across ~100,000 trials under a novel seven-tier contextual escalation ladder, crossing occupational details with organizational pressure. Our findings reveal that LLMs' compliance with employment discrimination law is brittle. More

MATS Dec 7, 2026

Uncovering Hidden Propensities in Language Models via Limited-Parameter Finetuning

Uncovering Hidden Propensities in Language Models via Limited-Parameter Finetuning Large language models can have hidden propensities: behaviours that arise only in rare contexts. These hidden propensities pose a challenge to alignment audits, which seek to uncover problematic model behaviour but are limited in scope to scenarios anticipated by the auditor. To address this challenge, we explore limited-parameter finetuning (LPF) on examples of a target behaviour. While training 3–15% of model parameters, LPF reveals whether the model has a hidden propensity, even when standard behavioural evaluations cannot. LPF succeeds on a suite of model organisms with hidden propensities (8B-14B parameters), and also surfaces naturally occurring hidden propensities in open-weight models (27-32B parameters). LPF can thus distinguish between behaviourally equivalent models, even without knowing the context in which the propensity surfaces. LPF provides a novel affordance that may be useful for auditing frontier AI models. More

"It's Just a Small Weapon": What Rewarding Misbehavior During RL Does to Character-Trained Models

"It's Just a Small Weapon": What Rewarding Misbehavior During RL Does to Character-Trained Models Developers train models to follow a consistent character using methods like Model Spec Midtraining (Li et al., 2026), which have been hypothesized to make alignment more robust and generalize broadly. However, it is possible for subsequent RL with misspecified rewards to work against character training. In this work, we apply character training to teach gpt-oss-20b a semi-realistic constitution and study how it interacts with two types of RL that reward breaking the constitution's rules (opposing RL): answering harmful requests rewritten as math problems, and reward hacking in agentic coding environments. The character-trained models learn both behaviors easily, though they are slightly slower than gpt-oss-20b to answer harmful requests. Character-trained models also display more motivated reasoning on harmful answers than gpt-oss-20b, and hack on some tasks which monitors find harder to catch. More

Say Shibboleth: Fingerprinting the LLMs and Harnesses Behind Agentic Attacks

Say Shibboleth: Fingerprinting the LLMs and Harnesses Behind Agentic Attacks We study the fingerprinting of agentic cyberattacks. Our objective is to reliably infer information about the attacker's configuration, such as LLM version and harness, from telemetry. To achieve this, we introduce a technique we call shibboleth traps: modular honeypot services designed to elicit discriminative behaviors from agentic attackers. The traps make informative interactions a condition for obtaining resources the agent seeks, eliciting model declarations and characteristic writing without requiring a successful prompt injection. To achieve harness fingerprinting, we introduce general mechanisms to indirectly probe peculiar execution behaviors of harnesses. These probes reveal how a harness masks secrets in tool output, enforces command time limits, and schedules tool calls, using only the effects visible in subsequent requests. More

Can Mechanistic Interpretability Help Explain LLM Agent Failures? A Case Study on Web Element Hallucination

Mohamed Aghzal, Mor Geva, Gregory J. Stein, Ziyu Yao

Mechanistic Interpretability (MI) has made significant progress in explaining the internal computations of Large Language Models (LLMs); however, these advances have yet to demonstrate comparable success in explaining the decision-making processes of LLM-based agents. This paper examines the challenges MI tools face when applied to realistic agentic failures. We focus on one such failure: web agents hallucinating actions that are not available on a website. We apply a series of mechanistic analyzes to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as it interacts with live websites. We find that a linear probe trained on the last prompt token can predict hallucinations before generation begins (0.80 AUC) and steering along a probe direction corrects 85\% of hallucinations, but disrupts 7\% of valid actions. However, these results only provide a partial explanation of why the agent hallucinates. More

Causal AI-Driven Secure mmWave Beam Alignment with Defensive Variational Autoencoders

Nasir Khan, Asmaa Abdallah, Abdulkadir Celik, Ahmed M. Eltawil, Sinem Coleri, Cicek Cavdar

Efficient and secure beam alignment is essential for mmWave communication systems. However, existing deep learning (DL)-based beam alignment methods often neglect causal relationships between inputs and outputs, resulting in limited interpretability, poor generalization, and excessive beam sweeping. Moreover, these DL solutions are vulnerable to adversarial attacks, where small perturbations in received power measurements can mislead beam classifiers. To overcome these issues, we propose a causally-aware DL framework integrating causal discovery into beam alignment. A two-stage causal beam selection algorithm learns a Bayesian graph capturing dependencies between received signal strength indicators (RSSI) as inputs and the optimal beam index as the target variable, then uses it to select a minimal set of relevant input features for the DL classifier. To mitigate adversarial attacks, we introduce a defensive variational autoencoder (DVAE) that maps potentially perturbed inputs back to their clean versions. More

Interpretable weight-space programming builds transparent transformers and brain encoding models

Yash Mehta, Chandan Singh, Weijia Xu, Richard J. Antonello, Adeen Flinker, Nima Mesgarani, Michael F. Bonner, Nebojsa Jojic, Jianfeng Gao

Neural networks are powerful predictors, yet their opacity limits their usefulness in science, where the goal is explanation rather than mere prediction. In neuroscience, for example, deep encoding models accurately predict brain activity in response to natural stimuli, but struggle to reveal which stimulus features drive those predictions. Here we introduce interpretable weight-space programming (iWSP), a method in which an LLM agent iteratively builds interpretable models by explicitly programming their weights and features in code, without gradient-based training (backpropagation). Without backpropagation to tune parameters, the agent must draw on its prior knowledge and reason over previous trials to hypothesize, hand-craft, and refine human-interpretable computations mirroring the workflow of a human scientist. LLM audits confirm the interpretability of the hand-crafted models. More

DECODE: Diagnosing Interpretability Failures in Automated Interpretation Process

Dohyun Kim, Jaeung Lee, Donghee Hong, Jaemin Jo

Tools for interpreting the internal representations of Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly rely on LLMs themselves to both generate and evaluate explanations, creating a self-referential automated interpretation process with minimal human oversight. This automation raises significant reliability concerns, which are exacerbated by the absence of ground truth. In practice, it is difficult to validate explanations or to attribute interpretability failures to their source -- whether they originate from the tool's extracted representations or from the LLM used in the process. To address these challenges, we present DECODE, a visual analytics system that reintroduces human oversight into the automated interpretation process. Our approach decomposes the diagnostic workflow into three progressive stages, isolating failure sources ranging from structural defects to explanation-level misalignments. Drawing inspiration from inter-coder consensus in qualitative analysis, we utilize consensus among independently generated explanations and their evaluation metrics as a proxy for reliability. More

V-JEPA-LM: Video-Language Models That Read Dynamics Beyond Static, Text-Aligned Representations

Keuntae Kim, Dongjun Kim, Yong Suk Choi

World-model video encoders such as V-JEPA learn how scenes change (direction, speed, order, state) from video alone. To answer questions, they are joined to a language model trained on captions and question-answer pairs. But each caption or question names only a few attributes of a clip, so the model learns to retrieve those attributes rather than to read the clip, and the dynamics the encoder carries go unused: on the same items, a linear probe reads speed from frozen V-JEPA features at 87% while the VLM answers at chance. We show where dynamics lives in a frozen encoder: in a subspace shared across videos for predictive encoders such as V-JEPA, but not as a shared component of frame-wise, text-aligned encoders such as SigLIP. Interpreting Coding Agents Through Their Behavioral Trajectories

Ibragim Badertdinov, Alexander Golubev

Standard coding-agent benchmarks primarily evaluate binary task success, treating execution as a black box and obscuring the behaviors that produce those outcomes. We analyze a large corpus of 143,209 execution trajectories from 125 agent configurations spanning 117 unique models. To enable comparisons across heterogeneous agent interfaces, we develop a rule-based parser that maps raw execution traces to high-level actions such as repository search, file editing, and test execution, and audit its predictions against a six-model consensus reference on 500 events. We find that agents with similar solve rates can nevertheless differ substantially in how they explore, modify and verify code. Moreover, without using task outcomes, token counts, or tool names as features, aggregate behavioral profiles retrieve the corresponding agent stack across disjoint repository sets and programming languages, although task composition is not fully controlled in the cross-repository setting. In practice, many deployers rely on system prompting to implement contextual norms and guardrails at inference time. Prior work has shown that post-generation reflective reasoning with critique and revision can improve conformance compared to simple system prompting, but such methods are limited to the reasoning abilities inherent in the trained model.
