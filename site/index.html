“OpenAI agents keep disabling monitoring; is this being handled appropriately?” by Alec Harris

Redwood Research Blog

Subtitle: Partly, but monitoring weak points and a lack of transparency about which cases reach human review remain concerning. Recently, OpenAI disclosed two incidents in which their agents suppressed or disabled misalignment monitoring. These are part of a broader pattern: OpenAI's agents “keep turning off misalignment monitoring” when working on code that runs other models (the agents are not turning off their own monitoring; they are turning off the monitoring on the models they are running). This is happening at such a large volume that OpenAI feels it is impractical for the security team to be immediately paged for these cases. To their credit, OpenAI's disclosure was fast and candid, and they are rolling out mitigations to make monitoring harder to turn off. But those mitigations are unlikely to work every time, and some of these incidents have already ended with an AI actually running unmonitored. More

“Towards understanding score-seeking in LLMs” by Alex Mallen, Aghyad Deeb, Ryan Greenblatt

Redwood Research Blog

Subtitle: Models including GPT-5.6 Sol appear to reward-hack because this increases their score; and recent models also care about increasing the score of their peer agents. The field of AI alignment understands surprisingly little about the details of AIs’ current misaligned motivations. Production LLMs sometimes reward-hack, exploiting flaws in how tasks are scored rather than performing the tasks as intended, and we’ve recently seen AIs reward-hack in some particularly egregious ways. But we don’t really understand what motivates them. When AIs reward-hack, what are they trying to achieve? Our new paper, led by Aghyad Deeb, tries to pin this down empirically for GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.5, and a checkpoint of OpenAI o3 (“exp-rl-cap-late”) without safety training, in a handful of agentic environments with an exposed grader. In our environments, these models appear to reward-hack because reward-hacking improves their score. We call their desire to score well according to graders “score-seeking”. More

MATS Dec 7, 2026

Uncovering Hidden Propensities in Language Models via Limited-Parameter Finetuning

Uncovering Hidden Propensities in Language Models via Limited-Parameter Finetuning Large language models can have hidden propensities: behaviours that arise only in rare contexts. These hidden propensities pose a challenge to alignment audits, which seek to uncover problematic model behaviour but are limited in scope to scenarios anticipated by the auditor. To address this challenge, we explore limited-parameter finetuning (LPF) on examples of a target behaviour. While training 3–15% of model parameters, LPF reveals whether the model has a hidden propensity, even when standard behavioural evaluations cannot. LPF succeeds on a suite of model organisms with hidden propensities (8B-14B parameters), and also surfaces naturally occurring hidden propensities in open-weight models (27-32B parameters). LPF can thus distinguish between behaviourally equivalent models, even without knowing the context in which the propensity surfaces. LPF provides a novel affordance that may be useful for auditing frontier AI models. More

Generative Latent Priors for Interpreting and Steering Protein Language Models

Daniel Hinjos, Lucía Urcelay

Dominant methods for interpreting large models impose strong structural assumptions. Generative Latent Priors (GLP) are a complementary approach: a diffusion model that learns the distribution of a frozen model's activations directly, imposing no assumptions. We port GLP to ESM-2 activations (UniRef50) and evaluate it at two host scales (ESM-2-8M and -650M), both as a lens for protein representations and as a prior for editing them. Generation quality scales smoothly with depth and compute. As a lens, single GLP "meta-neurons" are the strongest probes of discrete Swiss-Prot concepts at both hosts, beating InterPLM's released SAE features, and lead on continuous properties at 8M though not at 650M; falsification tests show this is mostly concentration, not new information. Alignment by Confident Debate: Preference Learning from Multi-Agent Debate

Nikhil Verma, Dingxian Wang, Mohammad Reza Rezaei, Andrew Rabinovich, Zhao Chen

Post-training language models for reasoning typically rely on gold answers, task-specific verifiers, or learned reward models, limiting scalability when such signals are scarce. Multi-agent debate offers a self-supervised alternative, but converting debate into reliable training supervision remains challenging: majority voting weights agreeing responses equally, lacks a principled tie-breaking rule, and is ill-defined for open-ended tasks without a canonical answer; likelihood-based scoring can conflate fluency with correctness; and homogeneous committees may reinforce correlated errors. We introduce Alignment by Confident Debate (AbCD), which converts debate traces into preference data without gold answer labels. A sampling-diverse committee debates each problem, after which final answers are clustered by task-level semantic equivalence. More

Interpretable Context-Aware Multi-Label Retinal Classification with Dual-Hub Heterogeneous Graph Neural Network

Proshanto Chanda, Munib Mesinovic, Tingting Zhu, Abhirup Banerjee

Multi-label retinal screening requires capturing co-occurring pathologies and clinical context, yet standard multimodal fusion remains opaque. We propose a dual-hub heterogeneous graph neural network (GNN) that couples spatial fundus features and structured clinical covariates through cross-modal message passing while providing inherent interpretability. Rather than relying on post-hoc saliency approximations, our architecture exposes multi-level decision pathways during forward aggregation: spatially biased self-attention captures lesion-level patch interactions, while modality-specific graph attention coefficients explicitly quantify the contribution of visual regions and clinical risk factors to each disease node. Evaluated on the BRSET dataset (16,266 photographs from 8,524 patients), the proposed model achieves 76.1% Macro-AUPRC across seven conditions, matching or exceeding image-only (75.9%) and ResNet-50+MLP fusion (75.9%) baselines, with largest contextual gains in macular edema (+3.5%) and diabetic retinopathy (+2.1%). Codebase is available at: https://github.com/proshanto-c/ca-mlc-refactor. More

Controllable Molecule Generation via Sparse Representation Editing: An Interpretability-Driven Perspective

Zhuoran Li, Xu Sun, Chang Wen Chen, Wanyu Lin

Controllable molecule generation is crucial for diverse scientific applications, such as drug discovery and materials design. While large language models (LLMs) show great promise, their dense and entangled representations impede precise control over the generation of molecules with bespoke substructures or properties. To address this, we propose Sparse Representation Editing (SpaRE), an interpretability-driven framework for fine-grained and precise control in LLM-based molecule generation. The crux of SpaRE is to learn an overcomplete sparse feature space that disentangles LLM representations into a compact set of latent features corresponding to chemically meaningful concepts. Within this space, we can directly manipulate these concept-aligned latent features to achieve (1) local control, by generating target atoms and functional groups at specified positions; and (2) global control, by customizing the overall structural and physicochemical properties within defined ranges. In this way, our framework advances interpretability from post-hoc analysis to actionable generative control. More

Beyond the Grid: Continuous Dictionary Pursuit for Interpretable Signal Decomposition

Nick Janssen, Melanie Schaller, Bodo Rosenhahn

Deep learning models achieve high accuracy in signal processing but remain difficult to interpret. Classical matching pursuit and sparse coding offer better transparency but suffer from spectral leakage and grid mismatch when signal frequencies do not align with discrete dictionary atoms. We introduce Continuous Dictionary Pursuit (CDP), a framework for interpretable signal decomposition that learns the parameters of closed-form functions directly from data. CDP optimizes over a continuous manifold of differentiable atoms and, unlike prior continuous pursuit methods that operate on a single analytic family, searches a heterogeneous library spanning periodic, trend, transient, and discontinuous structures. The algorithm employs a greedy iterative strategy using data-driven priors and gradient-based optimization to isolate individual signal components, with discontinuous waveforms made differentiable through spectral sharpness annealing. To ensure a sparse atom set for reconstruction, the framework utilizes a stopping criterion based on a predefined atom budget and residual error convergence. More

Language, Not Code Abstraction: Natural-language Training Shapes Brain-aligned Geometry

Iñigo Parra

Code-trained language models produce positive brain scores, which raises the question of whether abstract structure learned from programming languages can substitute for natural-language training. We trained 25 GPT-2-small models on TinyStories, Python, C functions, x86-64 assembly, and the corresponding machine code, and scored them using a shared tokenizer and encoding procedure against matched random-weight models. On Pereira-243 and Tuckute2024, TinyStories exceeds the mean of the four code models by $Δr = 0.053$ and $0.059$ (Holm-corrected $p<0.001$), while code training leaves alignment near the random-weight level. The language advantage is benchmark-specific: Blank2014 shows no corrected language-code difference, and Fedorenko2016 shows no language benefit. Geometry tracks the encoding result on Pereira-243: TinyStories seeds converge on the most similar representations, and its fit-free correspondence with neural dissimilarity peaks at $ρ= 0.14$, twice the code and random-weight levels. Learning from How Users Interpret: Personalizing Recommendation Explanation Order

Deo Munduku, Elsa Negre

Personalized explanations typically adapt their content or presentation based on preferences, interaction histories, or other user characteristics. We examine whether regularities inferred from prior textual interpretations can support personalization of explanation organization. In a controlled within participant study with 122 participants, prior interpretations were represented along three dimensions: information selection, relative salience, and relational organization. The relational dimension was partially operationalized through co mobilization. These representations were aggregated into fixed historical profiles and used to organize otherwise identical explanation sentences. Participants encountered four organization procedures: the OWN historical profile, the DONOR historical profile, a population profile called POP, and a language model procedure using the historical profile called PROFILE LLM. Post exposure objective comprehension was the primary outcome. Vicky Jung, Claire M. He, Irene Chang, Genevera I. Allen

Dimensionality reduction is widely used to visualize high-dimensional data and guide scientific discovery, yet interpreting which original features contribute to low-dimensional representations remains challenging, particularly for black-box methods such as t-SNE and UMAP. We introduce \textbf{MEDALLION} (Manifold Embedding Distillation and Attributions for Low-dimensional Latent InterpretatiON), a novel framework for interpreting low-dimensional embeddings through knowledge distillation and neural feature attribution. User Embeddings are Superpositions of Interpretable Behavioral Modes

Jonathan Kozaczuk, Zheng Dong, Noyan Evirgen, Keivan Majidi, Eddie K. H. Ng, Chris Porter, Qi Zhao, Jia Geng, Changmao Li, Brandon R. Feng

User embeddings are central components of modern recommendation, personalization, and behavioral modeling systems. Though these embeddings can compactly encode rich behavioral information, they are inherently dense and opaque, limiting interpretability. To address this gap, we argue that user embeddings can be understood as sparse superpositions of interpretable behavioral modes. This structure is directly predicted by generative models of user behavior for linear embeddings and extends approximately to deep-learning representations by the superposition hypothesis. Superimposed modes can naturally be recovered by sparse dictionary learning methods, such as sparse autoencoders (SAEs), and recovered modes can be automatically named and queried in natural language. We validate mode recovery against known ground truth on synthetic data, where SAEs recover modes better than classical sparse baselines, including on transformer-encoded embeddings. We then apply the method to classical and transformer-based user embeddings from two real-world datasets. More

Towards interpreting any residual stream direction by scaling activation inversion

Celeste De Schamphelaere, Juan J. Vazquez, Ari Spiesberger, Tomáš Gavenčiak, Gavin Leech, Euan Ong, Jiahai Feng

To interpret a direction in a language model's residual stream, we can look for text that strongly activates it. But when such text is found by scanning a corpus, it is limited to what that corpus contains. We develop Maximally-Activating Example Metamodels (MAEMs), language models which map residual stream directions from a subject model to text exemplars that strongly activate those directions. Our objective is to maximise the cosine similarity of a supplied residual stream direction with the activations of the text generated by the MAEM. This label-free RL objective helps to scale MAEMs on diverse sources of residual stream directions. Empirically, we find the resultant metamodel produces informative exemplars: a Qwen3.6-27B MAEM inverts held-out activations better than corpus search or a natural-language autoencoder (NLA), beats corpus search (at 10M tokens) on a majority of held-out languages/domains, and activates 89% of held-out SAE features. Value-oriented policy distillation for interpretable wind farm power control

Max Bruninx, Timothy Verstraeten, Jan Decuyper, Ann Nowé, Jan Helsen

Wind farm power control can help the system maintain balance and avoid blackouts. In Europe, this can be achieved through implicit balancing, where a wind farm adjusts its power setpoint in anticipation of the imbalance price using information published by the Transmission System Operator. Thereby, the wind farm helps restore system balance while also reducing its own imbalance costs resulting from forecast errors. However, implicit balancing is a challenging problem since the settlement price is unknown in advance and can be impacted by the pursued control strategy. Reinforcement learning methods could help to solve this problem, but lack the interpretability needed to be deployed on real wind farms. Therefore, we propose a policy distillation framework using explainable boosting machines (EBMs), a variant of generalized additive models, to obtain a transparent control policy. More

Generate-then-Rank: Learning Symbolic Goal Interpretation for Embodied Agents

Jisen Lei, Huizhi Liang

Goal interpretation is to translate a natural language instruction into a formal symbolic goal that a planner can execute. It is critical for embodied agents because any error here propagates to every downstream planning and action step. Doing it well requires both generating suitable candidates and reliably selecting among them. We present a two-stage generate-then-rank approach to Goal Interpretation in the Embodied Agent Interface (EAI). First, supervised fine-tuning adapts a Large Language Model (LLM) to EAI's output convention. Second, Candidate-Output Ranking from Errors (CORE) learns preferences over complete outputs from the model's own generated alternatives. We conducted experiments on the household scenes of the Embodied Agent Interface Benchmark. Multiple open source LLMs including DeepSeek-V3 chat, DeepSeek-Reasoner, and Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-72B were evaluated. The proposed method outperformed the baseline models including LLMs with simple prompting, chain-of-thought, and knowledge graph based approaches. More

EnergyBench: a live, gate-aligned benchmark of day-ahead forecasts for European power markets

Evgenii Genov, Ander Martinez Alonso, Christoph Bergmeir

Energy forecasting models are usually compared on backtests and fixed public datasets. Backtests can use data that did not yet exist when the forecast would have been made, and a fixed test set, reused across studies and possibly contained in the pretraining data of foundation models, invites overfitting to the benchmark. EnergyBench evaluates every entry live instead. Each day at 11:30 CET/CEST, thirty minutes before the day-ahead (SDAC) gate closes, every model forecasts the next day's price, wind and solar generation and load on the 15-minute grid for six bidding zones (BE, NL, FR, DE-LU, DK1, DK2). Unlike existing live benchmarks, EnergyBench runs the models itself, on one information set that it freezes at the cut-off and publishes, including three numerical weather prediction models, so that foundation models can be compared with and without the same weather covariates under the actual market deadline. Pengpeng Qiao, Yang Cao, Lingling Zhang, Guo Cheng, Junwei Chen, Manjiang Yu, Wei Yang Bryan Lim, Masatoshi Yoshikawa

Federated fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs) under heterogeneous client capacities is increasingly important, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) with heterogeneous ranks makes it practical. Existing server-side aggregators compose local matrix updates $ΔW_i$ from client-specific LoRA factors $(A_i,B_i)$ before aggregating them. Although these matrix updates lie in the same weight space $\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and can be averaged directly, the LoRA factors that generate them may expressed client-specific local basis. In the typical compose--aggregate--factorize--dispatch loop, this basis inconsistency can make the rank-$r_i$ reference prefix dispatched to lower-rank clients unstable after server-side refactorization. In this work, we propose \textbf{FedAbA}, a basis-consistent federated LoRA aggregation framework for heterogeneous ranks. FedAbA first extracts each client's local basis, aligns it to the corresponding prefix of the global reference basis, and constructs a basis-consistent reconstruction of each local matrix update before aggregation. When Scores Conflict with Preferences: Pre-Alignment Diagnosis and Adaptive Correction of Constraint Drift in DPO

Ping Liu, Yan Yan

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is increasingly used in settings where pairwise preferences coexist with pointwise criteria such as response length, answer correctness, or safety scores. When the two disagree, standard DPO treats pairs whose preferred output scores worse on the pointwise criterion (conflict pairs) in the same way as the remaining (matching) pairs. We characterize a systematic failure mode, constraint drift: for sufficiently small updates, conflict pairs tend to increase constraint cost while matching pairs tend to repair it, and the net change depends on their balance. This balance yields a closed-form pre-alignment diagnostic, $ρ^{*}_{\mathrm{eq}}$, computable from dataset statistics, that indicates whether a dataset is self-correcting or requires intervention. Building on this diagnostic, we propose Conflict-aware DPO (CoDPO), which down-weights conflict pairs with a strength set from $ρ^{*}_{\mathrm{eq}}$ rather than tuned, and adds an adaptive margin shift that addresses residual drift during training. Current generative AI alignment relies on a myopic paradigm that prioritizes immediate conversational satisfaction. By relying on short-horizon feedback, this approach ignores the profound, longitudinal externalities of persistent human-AI interaction, which can lead to adverse outcomes such as cognitive atrophy, social erosion, and diminished human agency. To address this, we propose a paradigm shift toward long-horizon alignment that accounts for how AI reshapes human experience over time. Addition by Subtraction: Toward Mitigating Fragile Safety Alignment in Large Language Models

Hamid Dashtbani, Alexandra Dmitrienko

Large language models (LLMs) undergo safety alignment to prevent harmful generations, yet they remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. Recent work reveals a deeper structural weakness: safety alignment can be highly sparse, such that pruning a small number of safety-critical neurons or ablating a single refusal direction can substantially undermine aligned behavior. In this work, we take the first steps toward strengthening safety alignment such that it is less dependent on sparse model components. We propose Addition by Subtraction, which gradually removes identified safety-critical neurons during finetuning and uses preference optimization to re-establish safety-aligned behavior through other parts of the model. Our results show that the resulting models are substantially more robust to pruning-based attacks. Remarkably, these benefits generalize beyond the white-box attacks motivating our design: Addition by Subtraction improves robustness against other sparsity-based interventions, including refusal-direction ablation, as well as a broad range of black-box jailbreak attacks. Interpretable Text Embeddings Based on Hierarchical Questions

Kohei Oda, Po-Min Chuang, Kiyoaki Shirai, Natthawut Kertkeidkachorn

QA-based text embeddings represent texts as vectors consisting of 1/0 answers to yes/no questions. Although these embeddings provide detailed information for interpretation and achieve high task performance, they often require humans to examine a large number of questions. Furthermore, the relationship between the number of questions and the appropriate level of question granularity has not been sufficiently studied. To address these challenges, we propose a method for constructing hierarchical questions for text embeddings. As the hierarchy deepens, questions become more specific and their number increases. Does Context Compression Preserve Refusal Alignment?

Anthony Nguyen, Wenjun Lin

Context compression reduces inference cost by encoding inputs into compact representations while preserving semantic content. An open question is whether semantic preservation alone is sufficient to maintain downstream behaviours such as refusal alignment. We investigate this question and find that encoder-based compression systematically weakens refusal behaviour in instruction-tuned language models, despite high reconstruction fidelity. This effect persists across model families and compression architectures. Mechanistic analysis shows that compression attenuates activation along the decoder's learned refusal direction. We further explore Memory Steering, a lightweight inference-time intervention that restores refusal rates to near-baseline levels without retraining and operates entirely in compressed representation space. These results demonstrate that semantic preservation does not guarantee behavioural preservation under compression, highlighting the need to explicitly preserve alignment-relevant features in compression-aware systems.
